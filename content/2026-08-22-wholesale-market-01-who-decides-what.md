Ask who regulates electricity in El Salvador and the answer depends on when you
asked. Until July of this year it was the Superintendencia General de
Electricidad y Telecomunicaciones. Since 2 July 2026 it is the Dirección
General de Energía, Hidrocarburos y Minas, and SIGET keeps its acronym while
losing the word that made it relevant to this sector.

That is not a footnote. It is the kind of change that invalidates every
institutional diagram published before this year, including most of what a
search engine will hand you.

> A sector diagram is a snapshot of a legal arrangement, not a description of
> physics. The energy still flows the same way. The authority over it does not.

This is the first article in a series on the Salvadoran wholesale electricity
market — the rules, the price formation mechanism, and the operational
processes that sit underneath them. It starts with the map, because every
subsequent article depends on knowing which entity issues what.

## Four functions, and who holds them

Unbundling is the organising idea. Before the reform of the late nineties, a
single state entity generated, transmitted, distributed and set the terms.
The reform separated those into activities that could be competitive
(generation, commercialisation) and activities that are natural monopolies
(transmission, distribution), and then created institutions to police the
boundary.

Four functions have to be held by someone:

| Function | Holder | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Sector policy | DGEHM | Absorbed the former Consejo Nacional de Energía |
| Regulation and supervision | DGEHM since July 2026 | Previously SIGET |
| System operation and market administration | Unidad de Transacciones | Private company, S.A. de C.V. |
| Transmission ownership and maintenance | ETESAL | 115 kV and 230 kV |

The distinction that matters most for anyone working inside the market is the
one between the third row and the second. The UT operates the system and
administers the market. It does not regulate it. When the UT publishes an
indexation or validates a declared cost, it is applying a rule someone else
approved — and when a participant disputes that application, the dispute
climbs to the regulator, not to the UT.

The UT began operating in 1998 and is constituted as a private company whose
board seats the market participants themselves — generators, transmitters,
distributors, commercialisers and end users — alongside consumer and policy
representation. That governance structure is unusual and worth understanding
properly rather than assuming.

## The reform of July 2026

Worth setting out precisely, because it is recent enough that most published
material has not caught up.

The Dirección General de Energía, Hidrocarburos y Minas was created by
Legislative Decree No. 190 of 26 October 2021, published in the Diario Oficial
No. 212, Volume 433, on 8 November 2021, entering into force one year after
publication. That decree dissolved the Consejo Nacional de Energía and the
Ministry of Economy's hydrocarbons and mining directorate, transferring their
powers to the new body.

That first step consolidated *policy*. Regulation stayed with SIGET.

On 2 July 2026 the Legislative Assembly approved, with 57 votes, reforms to
both founding laws that move the electricity *regulatory* function to the DGEHM
as well. The operative details:

- Regulatory, administrative and registry powers over electricity transfer to
  the DGEHM, along with files, archives, assets and resources.
- The transition has a maximum term of six months, coordinated by a special
  commission. Counting from the approval date, that runs into early 2027.
- Any reference to SIGET in electricity legislation, contracts or agreements is
  to be read as a reference to the DGEHM.
- SIGET is renamed Superintendencia General de Telecomunicaciones, keeps the
  acronym, and retains only telecommunications competences.
- Judicial proceedings already under way stay with SIGET; administrative ones
  transfer.

The practical consequence for anyone reading the regulatory corpus right now is
that the documents say SIGET and mean DGEHM, and that the transition is still
running as of this writing.

## Two markets under one roof

The wholesale market is not a single trading venue. It is two, both administered
by the UT.

The **Mercado de Contratos** is forward. Participants negotiate bilaterally,
independently of the UT, and the UT dispatches the result. Terms — quantity,
duration, price — belong to the parties.

The **Mercado Regulador del Sistema** is short-term. It is the spot market, and
it clears the difference between what was contracted and what was actually
needed. Every hour, someone consumed more or less than their contracts covered,
and some generator produced more or less than it committed. The MRS settles
that gap at a price that varies hour by hour.

The relationship is worth stating carefully, because it is a common source of
confusion: a contract is a financial arrangement between two parties. It does
not determine which machine runs. Dispatch is decided centrally, on economic
merit, regardless of who has a contract with whom. A generator with a contract
may not be dispatched; a generator without one may run all day. The contract
determines who pays whom, and the MRS reconciles the difference between the
contractual position and the physical one.

## Who participates

Market participants fall into five categories: generators, transmitters,
distributors, commercialisers, and end users connected directly to the
transmission network. Each transacts in the wholesale market through the UT.

The distribution side is concentrated in a handful of companies serving defined
territories. The generation side is more fragmented, and its composition has
shifted materially over the last few years with the entry of large natural-gas
capacity and a growing share of solar.

## Why costs and not offers

This is the design choice that shapes everything in the rest of the series, and
it is the one most worth understanding as a principle rather than as a rule.

In an offer-based market, generators submit prices freely. The market operator
stacks the offers, and the intersection with demand sets the price. The
discipline comes from competition: offer too high and you do not get
dispatched. It works when there are enough independent competitors that no
single one can move the price, and when the marginal technology is one whose
costs are difficult to hide.

In a cost-based market, generators do not offer a price. They declare their
costs — fuel price, efficiency, variable non-fuel costs — and the operator
computes the merit order from those declared costs. The discipline comes from
verification: the declaration has to be documented and auditable, and the
operator validates it against approved references.

The trade-off is straightforward. An offer-based market has lower
administrative cost and relies on competition for discipline. A cost-based
market has high administrative cost — someone has to audit every generator and
validate every declaration — and relies on verification for discipline.

Small systems with concentrated generation and a marginal technology that runs
on imported fuel tend toward the second. With few competitors, competition
alone does not discipline offers. And when the marginal unit burns imported
fuel, its cost is externally observable, which makes verification feasible in a
way it would not be for, say, hydro with a reservoir.

El Salvador operates under the Reglamento de Operación del Sistema de
Transmisión y del Mercado Mayorista Basado en Costos de Producción — the ROBCP.
The name states the design: production costs, not offers. Energy prices in the
wholesale market are variable and the final price is set by the marginal cost,
hour by hour.

That last paragraph is the hinge of the whole series. If the price comes from
declared costs, then the integrity of the price depends entirely on the
integrity of the declarations — and validating declarations stops being an
administrative chore and becomes the mechanism by which the market works at
all.

## What this means for the rest of the series

The map above generates the questions the remaining articles have to answer.

If price is set by marginal cost, how is the merit order actually built, and
what breaks it? That is the next article.

If costs are declared rather than offered, what exactly does a generator
declare, how often, against what reference, and with what supporting
documentation? That is the core of the series, and the reason it exists.

If declarations are validated against approved structures, who approves those
structures, how often are they revised, and what happens between revisions?

And if the whole thing produces an hourly price, how does that price become a
monthly settlement — and where does the regional market fit in?

> The institutional map is not background. In a cost-based market, knowing who
> approves what is the difference between validating a declaration and merely
> receiving one.
